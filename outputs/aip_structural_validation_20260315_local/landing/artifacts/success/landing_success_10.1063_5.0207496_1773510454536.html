      Small molecule inhibitors (SMIs) have been gaining attention in the field of area-selective atomic layer deposition (ALD) because they can be applied in the vapor-phase. A major challenge for SMIs is that vapor-phase application leads to a disordered inhibitor layer with lower coverage as compared to self-assembled monolayers, SAMs. A lower coverage of SMIs makes achieving high selectivity for area-selective ALD more challenging. To overcome this challenge, mechanistic understanding is required for the formation of SMI layers and the resulting precursor blocking. In this study, reflection adsorption infrared spectroscopy measurements are used to investigate the performance of aniline as an SMI. Our results show that aniline undergoes catalytic surface reactions, such as hydrogenolysis, on a Ru non-growth area at substrate temperatures above 250 °C. At these temperatures, a greatly improved selectivity is observed for area-selective TaN ALD using aniline as an inhibitor. The results suggest that catalytic surface reactions of the SMI play an important role in improving precursor blocking, likely through the formation of a more carbon-rich inhibitor layer. More prominently, catalytic surface reactions can provide a new strategy for forming inhibitor layers that are otherwise very challenging or impossible to form directly through vapor-phase application.

      I. INTRODUCTION

      As device features shrink and their structures become increasingly complex, there is a need in the semiconductor industry for novel fabrication strategies with high control over directing matter to specific locations on a device structure.1,2 Conventionally, top-down patterning strategies involving repeated deposition, lithography, and etching steps have been employed to pattern semiconductor devices. However, lithography-based patterning typically leads to alignment issues (i.e., edge placement errors) and cannot always be used in complex 3D structures.3–5 As a result, bottom-up fabrication strategies are required to reliably manufacture certain state-of-the-art device structures, e.g., selective metal capping of Cu,6 the fully self-aligned via,7 and the bottomless via.8 Area-selective deposition (ASD) is a bottom-up fabrication strategy that exploits differences in surface chemistry on a previously defined pattern to selectively deposit material.9,10 This pattern consists of surfaces on which deposition is desired, referred to as the growth areas, and surfaces on which no deposition is desired, referred to as the non-growth areas. The selectivity S is a measure of the performance of an ASD process and is typically defined as
      S=θGA−θNGAθGA+θNGA≈tGA−tNGAtGA+tNGA,
      where θGA and θNGA are the coverages of the deposited material on the growth and non-growth areas, respectively. The selectivity can also be approximated by the amounts or thicknesses of deposited material on the growth (tGA) and non-growth areas (tNGA).9,11 Because ASD employs a previously defined pattern as a template for selective deposition, the deposited material is automatically aligned to that pattern, also known as self-aligned fabrication.12 

      Some material systems allow for ASD to occur directly without a functionalization step, i.e., inherent selectivity.13–17 In general, however, inhibitors are employed to enable ASD through selective functionalization of the non-growth area. Conventionally, self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) have been used as inhibitors.18–21 SAMs require a dense and well-ordered layer (i.e., relatively strong van der Waals interactions) in order to achieve high selectivity.22–24 However, the van-der-Waals interactions that are needed for high selectivity require large SAM monomers with, consequently, low vapor pressures, making vapor-phase application more challenging.25 In addition, due to the relatively large size of the SAM monomers, there are some concerns about whether SAMs will be compatible with the extremely small features of future semiconductor devices.26–29 

      During the past couple of years, the use of small molecule inhibitors (SMIs) for ASD has been gaining interest.28,30–33 SMIs are sub-nanometer molecules that can be dosed in the vapor-phase.9,25,34 Their small size allows them to be compatible with nanoscale features, while vapor-phase application makes SMIs more suitable for high-volume manufacturing. In addition, the vapor-phase application of the inhibitor allows the non-growth area to be (re)functionalized during the atomic layer deposition (ALD) process if required.9,25 Our strategy relies on re-applying the SMI at the start of every ALD cycle in an ABC-type ALD process, where steps A, B, and C comprise the inhibitor, precursor, and co-reactant doses, respectively. This strategy is compatible with the use of plasma or ozone as a co-reactant because the inhibitor is reapplied at the start of every ALD cycle.32,35–37 A challenge for SMIs, however, is that they form disordered inhibitor layers, leading to a relatively low inhibitor density. Random sequential adsorption (RSA) simulations38,39 show that the vapor-phase application of SMIs and the resulting random nature of their adsorption lead to a 20%–40% lower inhibitor density as compared to an ordered inhibitor layer; see Fig. 1. Because precursor blocking relies, in part, on the formation of a dense inhibitor layer,40 achieving high selectivity can be challenging when using SMIs. To overcome this challenge, mechanistic understanding is required for the formation of an SMI layer and its ability to block ALD precursor adsorption.

      In our previous work, the aromatic molecule aniline (C6H5NH2) was found to be very effective at blocking precursor adsorption on transition metal surfaces.32 In particular, on a Ru non-growth area, aniline is able to prevent TiN ALD for at least 200 ALD cycles, allowing for ∼6 nm of selective growth on a dielectric growth area. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations and random sequential adsorption (RSA) simulations have shown that the effective blocking by aniline can be attributed to a strong and relatively dense adsorption of aniline on the Ru surface, despite its vapor-phase application.39 Understanding the mechanisms responsible for the formation of the aniline inhibitor layer should offer valuable insights toward identifying other promising SMI candidates.

      In this article, the adsorption of aniline on the Ru non-growth area is studied on a mechanistic level using reflection absorption infrared spectroscopy (RAIRS, also known as IRRAS). In addition, we investigate how the adsorption mechanisms of aniline affect the selectivity of an area-selective ALD process. To this end, the above-mentioned area-selective TiN ALD process was expanded to a new material system, the area-selective deposition of TaN with metal-on-dielectric selectivity. TaN is a conductive nitride that has applications as a superconducting material in quantum devices,41–44 as thin film resistors,45 and as a diffusion barrier46–48 in the semiconductor industry. The results of this study suggest that catalytic surface reactions on the Ru non-growth area play an important role in achieving high selectivity at elevated substrate temperatures by forming a more effective inhibitor layer. Finally, it discusses how the observed catalytic surface reactions can be exploited in a new strategy for forming inhibitor layers during area-selective ALD processes.

      II. EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS AND COMPUTATIONAL METHODS

      The area-selective TaN depositions were performed in an Oxford Instruments FlexAL reactor. The reactor is equipped with an inductively coupled plasma (ICP) source, a 200 mm substrate table, and a turbo molecular pump backed by a roughing pump, leading to a base pressure of ∼10−6 mbar. In addition, the system has windows for in situ ellipsometry measurements that are protected by gate valves during deposition. The reactor is described in detail elsewhere.49 

      Area-selective TaN ALD was achieved using an ABC-type ALD cycle, where step A is a 10 × 500 ms aniline inhibitor dose, step B is a 4 s tert-butylimido-tris(dimethylamino)tantalum (TBTDMT) dose, and step C is a 10 s Ar/H2 plasma. Each 500 ms aniline (Sigma Aldrich, ReagentPlus, 99%, CAS number 62-53-3) pulse was performed with the butterfly valve to the pump closed to trap the aniline in the chamber, followed by a 10 s reaction step during which the butterfly valve remains closed and a 3 s purging step with the valve open. The TBTDMT (Strem Chemicals, Inc., >98%, CAS number 69039-11-8) precursor was dosed into the chamber using a 50 SCCM Ar flow as carrier gas. The aniline and TBTDMT bubblers were heated to 80 and 60 °C, respectively. During the Ar/H2 plasma, a 10 SCCM Ar and 40 SCCM H2 flow were used at 6 mTorr, employing 100 W RF power to strike the plasma.

      The nucleation curves were measured using a combination of in situ ellipsometry measurements and ex situ x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS); see Fig. S2. In situ spectroscopic ellipsometry was performed using a J.A. Woollam M2000D ellipsometer. The ellipsometry data were parameterized using one Drude and two Lorentz oscillators to represent the TaN film.50 XPS measurements were carried out on a K-Alpha system from Thermo Scientific. The Ta4f peak area was selected to determine the amount of TaN material on a surface using XPS. XPS spectra were measured with a 0.1 eV resolution, 50 eV pass energy, and 50 ms dwell time.

      To determine the N content after functionalizing a Ru non-growth area with aniline as a function of substrate temperatures, a single aniline dose (i.e., 10 × 500 ms) was applied to Ru substrates at four different substrate temperatures. In addition, a control sample without aniline dosing was prepared. The N content was determined by measuring the N1s peak (0.1 eV resolution, 150 eV pass energy, 100 scans, and 50 ms dwell time) with respect to the Ru 3p peak. The Ru 3d peak was not used for this analysis due to its overlap with the C 1s peak. Please note that the N content of the control sample is not equal to zero due to interactions with atmospheric species such as N2.51 

      In situ reflection adsorption infrared spectroscopy (RAIRS) experiments were performed on a home-built ALD tool; see Fig. 2. The home-built system is similar to the FlexAL system in terms of the plasma source and pumping system. In situ RAIRS can be performed through KBr windows, which are protected from deposition by gate valves. The measurements were performed using a Bruker Invenio source and a liquid N2 cooled mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector (Bruker Optics DigiTect MCT D313 detector). In contrast to the transmission IR measurements performed in previous work,52 RAIRS is only sensitive to vibrations that have an orthogonal component to the substrate’s surface.53–55

      The adsorption configurations predicted by DFT calculations in our previous work39 were used as a starting point for the vibrational analysis of IR spectra. The vibrational frequencies for these configurations were determined by density functional theory (DFT) calculations on cluster models; see Fig. S2. These cluster models were prepared based on the optimized adsorption geometries obtained from the periodic DFT simulations via the Vienna ab initio simulation package.56–58 The DFT calculations for predicting the IR vibrations were performed in Jaguar (the rapid ab initio electronic structure package of Schrödinger).59 B3LYP-D3 functionals were used with the LANDL2DZ basis set for Ru and the 6-31G** basis set for the rest of the elements. All frequencies were uniformly corrected for anharmonicity by a factor of 0.985. The peak intensities were first normalized to their respective CH stretching mode intensities and then corrected to only include the orthogonal component of the vibration at the Ru surface.

      III. RESULTS

      The studied area-selective ALD process for TaN employs a three-step (i.e., ABC-type) ALD cycle using aniline as the SMI (step A), tert-butylimidotris(dimethylamino)tantalum (TBTDMT) as the precursor (step B), and Ar–H2 plasma as the co-reactant (step C); see Fig. 3(a). Similar to our previous work with aniline as an inhibitor,32 immediate TaN growth was observed on dielectrics (i.e., Al2O3), while there is a nucleation delay on metal surfaces such as Ru; see Fig. 3(b). This observed nucleation behavior is in line with the selective adsorption of aniline on transition metal surfaces with respect to dielectric surfaces.32,39 Interestingly, the nucleation delay of the TaN process on the Ru non-growth area was found to be strongly dependent on the substrate temperature during area-selective ALD. At 175 °C, only ∼1 nm of selective TaN growth can be achieved with a high selectivity (better than 0.9)11 on Al2O3 with respect to Ru, whereas ∼3 nm of TaN can be deposited selectively using this process at 300 °C. Figure 3(c) shows the selectivity of the TaN process for 3 nm of deposition on the growth area, which is a typical thickness for TaN diffusion barriers in interconnect structures.60 Overall, the results show that the selectivity of the process for this target thickness is strongly dependent on the process temperature. A high selectivity (∼0.9) can be achieved at a substrate temperature of 250 °C and above, while below 250 °C the selectivity is less than 0.5. These results suggest that the properties of the inhibitor layer (e.g., areal density and chemical inertness toward the precursor) are different at elevated substrate temperatures.

      The adsorption of aniline on a Ru surface was studied using in situ RAIRS on a home-built ALD tool. RAIRS is compatible with metal substrates and is only sensitive to vibrations with an orthogonal component to the metal plane.53–55 This directionally dependent sensitivity of RAIRS causes some peaks to be enhanced or weakened depending on the orientation of the aniline with respect to the surface, which is taken into account in the peak assignment in Figs. 4(d) and 5(b). Figure 4(c) shows the RAIRS spectrum for aniline adsorption on Ru at a low substrate temperature (175 °C). The spectrum reveals that aniline adsorbs in a mixture of two adsorption configurations: the horizontal [Fig. 4(a)] and the vertical [Fig. 4(b)] configurations. In the horizontal configuration, the aniline binds with its phenyl ring to the surface, whereas in the vertical configuration, the aniline binds with the amine group to the Ru surface.39 The formation of a mixture of inhibitor adsorption configurations is expected from the surface functionalization literature61–67 and our previous work with acetylacetone (Hacac),52 highlighting the importance of considering all inhibitor adsorption configurations.

      IR peak assignment was based on DFT calculations [see Fig. 4(d)], performed for the horizontal and vertical configurations of aniline on Ru(0001) surfaces.39 The peak at 1620 cm−1 is attributed to a NH2 scissoring vibrational mode that has an orthogonal component to the surface for the horizontal configuration but is in-plane for the vertical configuration (see Fig. S3) and is, therefore, attributed to aniline adsorbed in the horizontal adsorption configuration. Vibrations at 1570, 1500, and 1320 cm−1 are attributed to several CH bending and CC stretching modes of the phenyl ring for aniline adsorbed in the vertical configuration. The same vibrations for the horizontal configuration should be largely parallel to the Ru surface and are red shifted with respect to those for the vertical configuration; see Fig. 4(d). Despite RAIRS being insensitive to vibrations parallel to the surface, the shoulder peaks between 1480 and 1350 cm−1 indicate that these vibrations can be detected to some degree, likely because the aniline is adsorbed at an angle to the surface. While the DFT calculations show that aniline adsorbs completely in the plane for the horizontal configurations, steric effects from high aniline surface coverage or roughness of the surface could slightly angle the aniline adsorbates. Finally, the peaks between 1350 and 1200 cm−1 are attributed to a NH2 wagging mode and a CN stretching mode for the vertical configuration.

      Figure 5 shows RAIRS spectra for aniline adsorption on a Ru non-growth area for a range of substrate temperatures. The RAIRS spectra show a clear difference in the adsorbed aniline layer for temperatures below and above 250 °C. Above 250 °C, all peaks attributed to NH2 or CN bonds are strongly decreased with respect to lower substrate temperatures, while the peak attributed to CH bending and CC stretching for a horizontal C6Hx ring (i.e., horizontal aniline, benzene, C6H5) at 1460 cm−1 is increased. As mentioned above, it should be noted that this vibration occurs largely parallel to the Ru surface according to the DFT calculations but is still detected because the C6Hx are likely adsorbed at a slight angle with the Ru surface. Taken together, the decrease in NH2 and CN peaks combined with an increase in C6Hx peaks at higher substrate temperatures suggest that so-called hydrogenolysis reactions are taking place on the Ru surface; see Fig. 6(a). In this reaction, the NH2 group dissociates from the aniline by reacting with H2 from the environment, resulting in adsorbed benzene or C6Hx species and volatile NH3; see Fig. 6. These observations were verified by ex situ XPS measurements that show a lower N content for Ru substrates functionalized with aniline at high substrate temperatures (i.e., above 200 °C) as compared to substrates functionalized at low substrate temperature; see Fig. S4. DFT calculations have shown that hydrogenolysis is favorable for aniline on a Ru surface if H2 or H is available.39 Considering that the co-reactant and pretreatment of the area-selective ALD process is an Ar–H2 plasma, hydrogenolysis can be expected to occur on the Ru non-growth area. The experimental observations are in line with the surface functionalization literature for aniline adsorption on Ni and Pt, which show that hydrogenolysis takes place between 200 and 300 °C, even without providing additional H2.68,69 Although it is difficult to distinguish between adsorbed benzene and dehydrogenated benzene in our RAIRS spectra, the literature suggests that benzene adsorbed on Ru should partially dehydrogenate at substrate temperatures above 150 °C,70,71 transforming the adsorbed benzene into C6Hx species; see Fig. 6(b).

      The onset temperature for the observed hydrogenolysis reaction coincides with the temperature above which the area-selective TaN ALD processes show strongly improved selectivity; see Fig. 3(c). These results, therefore, suggest that the catalytic surface reactions play an important role in precursor blocking. The catalytic surface reactions reduce the number of NH2 groups on the surface, which could potentially interact with the precursor through the nitrogen lone pair.25 Overall, we are left with a more carbon-rich layer as compared to the inhibitor layer without the catalytic surface reactions; see Figs. 6(c) and 6(d). Such carbonaceous layers have been shown to be effective at blocking precursor adsorption due to their stable structure without reactive sites.16,25,72,73 The transformation of the adsorbed aniline to a carbonaceous layer through catalytic surface reactions could, therefore, explain the improvement in selectivity observed at higher substrate temperatures.

      IV. DISCUSSION

      The catalytic surface reactions provide a new strategy for the formation of inhibitor layers. This reaction mechanism opens up opportunities to form inhibitor layers in situ on the non-growth area that comprise adsorbed species different from those dosed in the vapor-phase. The results also raise the question of whether aniline is required as the inhibitor for reaching high selectivity or whether any benzene derivative can be used to form the same carbonaceous layer. If so, the dosed inhibitor could be selected based on ease of handling, price, and high vapor pressure without significantly changing the resulting inhibitor layer. In addition, catalytic surface reactions provide the opportunity to have inhibitor species on the surface that are otherwise impractical or impossible to dose in the vapor-phase. For example, dehydrogenated benzene (i.e., C6H5) cannot simply be dosed in the vapor phase as an SMI. Finally, these reactions provide a knob to distinguish between different metal surfaces based on their catalytic activity. For example, if the growth area is a metal surface that is not catalytically active, a catalytically active metal surface could act as the non-growth area. Conversely, if the inhibitor adsorbs on metal surfaces but the reaction products from the catalytic reactions desorb from the surface, a catalytically active metal surface could be employed as a growth area.

      The main requirement for exploiting catalytic surface reactions to form an inhibitor layer that blocks ALD precursor adsorption is that the non-growth area is a catalytically active surface (e.g., Ru, Mo, and Co) onto which the vapor-phase inhibitor species can adsorb. In addition, the reaction products from the catalytic reaction should at least partially remain adsorbed on the surface in order to block precursor adsorption. It should be noted that catalysis typically refers to a catalytic surface reaction followed by the desorption of the reaction products, whereas in this work, the C6Hx species remain adsorbed on the surface, effectively poisoning the surface once it is completely covered. In the case of area-selective ALD, poisoning of the catalytic non-growth area by the SMI is desired in order to form an effective inhibitor layer. Therefore, a good starting point for identifying inhibitors that could behave as effectively as aniline in this work would be to test reactions from the catalysis literature that are known to poison or deactivate the catalytic surface. For example, the literature on catalyst deactivation through (self-limiting) coke formation on catalytic surfaces could provide interesting chemistries for establishing inhibitor layers through catalytic surface reactions.74–77 

      V. CONCLUSIONS

      Precursor blocking by aniline is strongly dependent on the substrate temperature of the Ru non-growth area. RAIRS experiments show that aniline adsorbs in a mixture of vertical and horizontal adsorption configurations. Aniline was found to undergo hydrogenolysis and potentially dehydrogenation reactions for the same substrate temperature range (above 250 °C), for which the studied area-selective TaN ALD process shows high selectivity. The results, therefore, suggest that the observed catalytic surface reactions play an important role in improving precursor blocking for the aniline inhibitor layer. The catalytic surface reactions convert the adsorbed aniline into carbonaceous species such as C6Hx while removing the more reactive NH2 groups. As a result, a more carbon-rich inhibitor layer is formed, which is likely more inert toward ALD precursor adsorption. Importantly, the observed catalytic surface reactions allow for the in situ generation of inhibitor species that are different from those that were dosed in the vapor-phase. Therefore, these reactions provide a new strategy for forming inhibitor layers, offering opportunities for the development of area-selective ALD with high selectivity.
