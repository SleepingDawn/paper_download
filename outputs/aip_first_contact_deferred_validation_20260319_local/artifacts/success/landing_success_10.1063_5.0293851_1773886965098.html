      We compute lattice thermal conductivities of Li 6PS 5X (X = Cl, Br, I) argyrodites from first principles within the Wigner transport formulation. We find that these systems are afflicted by unstable vibration of Li atoms off of their symmetric trigonal planar environment. We efficiently calculate effective temperature-dependent phonons using anharmonic renormalization. Good agreement with experimental conductivity measurements is obtained for the three materials. Like other argyrodite-class systems, these materials are found to possess very low, glassy lattice thermal conductivity that is nearly constant with temperature. The coherence effect is found to contribute comparably to the standard Boltzmann transport channel to the total conductivity. The study shows that the Wigner formalism is adapt at characterizing thermal conduction in solid electrolytes and ionic conductors.

      Topics
      Density functional theory, Thermal conductivity, Electrolytes, Phonon scattering

      I. INTRODUCTION

      Solid-state electrolytes (SSEs) are generally known to have low, glassy thermal conductivity1 owing to the soft vibrational dynamics of Li ions, which is what eases their migration and make them good ionic conductors. In turn, the poor dissipation of heat can lead easily to thermal runaway and poses a safety problem. Understanding their thermal transport properties is important for designing thermal management solutions for solid-state batteries. SSEs that are such poor conductors of heat include well known Na1+xZr2SixP3−xO12 (NASICONs),2 Li 3PS 4⁠,3 Li10GeP2S12 (LGPS), and argyrodites.4 

      Argyrodites are up-and-coming SSE for Li batteries known for good Li ion conductivity. Argyrodites containing Ag have recently been revealed to possess ultralow, glassy lattice thermal conductivities (⁠ κlat⁠)5 and confirmed by first-principles molecular dynamics simulations.6 The same is of Cu 7PS 6 and Cu 7PSe 6⁠.7,8 Li 6PS 5X, where X stands for halogen Cl, Br, or I, are also promising subclass of argyrodites. Their thermal transport properties have been measured experimentally4 but not computed from first principles.

      Previous computational studies of SSE κlat have relied on real-space molecular dynamics (MD) and Green–Kubo relations.3,6–8 This is the full-dynamics approach that is capable of describing thermal conductivity across all types of materials and lattice, including even nanotubes.9 One of the reasons surely is that glassy κlat is not amenable to straightforward Boltzmann transport equation (BTE) treatment, which is a reciprocal-space formalism relying on interatomic force constants (IFCs) for defining lattice waves (phonons) and perturbative scattering due to anharmonicity. Another reason is that if such soft systems of relatively large size contain imaginary phonon modes, extra care and work are needed to obtain effective temperature-dependent phonons to implement BTE. MD is a more straightforward approach in such cases, though the BTE approach could offer speed-up if implemented correctly and efficiently.

      In this work, we successfully compute κlat by employing state-of-the-art Wigner formulation that is capable of describing lattice thermal conduction at the glass limit beyond BTE, as well as efficient temperature-renormalization of imaginary phonons. Up to 600 K just below the melting temperature, we obtain good agreement with measured κlat⁠. So long as the structural complexity allows for efficient calculation and use of anharmonic IFCs, the Wigner formalism is found to be a sound approach to thermal conduction in ionic conductors.

      II. METHODS

      The route to obtaining phonons and their scattering rates is through interatomic force constants (IFCs). IFCs arise as the coefficients of lattice energy Taylor expansion with respect to displacements (⁠ u⁠),
      U=U0+12!∑abΦ2abuaub+13!∑abcΦ3abcuaubuc+14!∑abcdΦ4abcduaubucud+⋯,
      (1)
      whereby the second-order IFC (⁠ Φ2⁠) defines the harmonic phonon and higher-order IFCs (⁠ Φ≥3⁠) are anharmonicities. The IFCs are preliminarily fitted to a randomly atom-displaced set of training configurations of the 2×2×2 supercell whose forces are computed by density functional theory (DFT).10,11 All DFT calculations were done using Vienna ab initio Simulations Package (VASP)12–15 with Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof exchange-correlation functional16 modified for solids (PBEsol)17 and projected-augmented wave pseudopotentials.18 A rigorous comparative study of various functionals applied to rocksalt and zinc-blende structures has shown that,19 by mean relative absolute error, PBEsol is very accurate for κlat⁠.
      In an effort to better describe the imaginary modes and the strong anharmonicity, we introduce a second IFC fitting step using a more accurate ensemble of training configurations. To this end, we use the preliminary IFCs to generate the quantum covariance (QCV) matrix of displacements, which is defined for any harmonic IFC and the corresponding phonon representation as
      Σ=12MaMb∑νe^νa2nν+1ωνe^νb†,
      (2)
      where e^ are the eigenvectors, ω is the frequency, and n is the temperature-dependent Bose–Einstein population for phonon mode ν⁠. It describes the covariance of displacements of all pairs of atoms in their respective Cartesian directions. Using Σ⁠, we Gaussian-sample thermal displacements of atoms [ u∼N(0,Σ)], which generate a set of configurations with a more physical displacement profile for the materials at hand. Using these configurations as an augmented training set, we refit IFCs by Eq. (1), which results in more accurate IFCs, both harmonic and anharmonic. This step is critical for these systems because, without it, finite-temperature renormalization does not properly converge.
      Dynamically unstable materials can still be stabilized (frequencies become real) at higher temperatures by the fourth-order anharmonic potential as atoms explore increasingly larger displacements away from the equilibrium position. To correct this, we employ an efficient phonon renormalization scheme using anharmonic IFCs20,21 and obtain effective harmonic (EH) phonons at finite temperatures that are fully real. It starts by redistributing the fourth-order anharmonic forces to pairwise interactions. This results in effective second-order IFCs that lump the effects of the fourth-order potential,
      Φ2anh=A2−1(T)A4(T)Φ4.
      (3)
      This term is added to Φ2 such that
      Φ2EH=Φ2+Φ2anh
      (4)
      is the renormalized effective harmonic IFCs. Temperature dependence enters through A⁠, matrix of displacement products of all atoms in a configurational ensemble mapped onto all n-body clusters. The ensemble is sampled by Gaussian random sampling once again. Equations (2)–(4) are iterated until convergence of phonon frequencies. All of these processes are executed using HiPhive22 and in-house code modifications thereof for finite-temperature renormalization.
      Under the Peierls–Boltzmann transport equation (PBTE), only the diagonal components of the velocity tensors (⁠ v⁠) contribute to κlat⁠. Though successful for crystalline solids where phonon quasiparticle transport is dominant, PBTE alone does not capture the diffusive thermal transport in highly disordered and anharmonic amorphous systems, which are, in turn, better described by an Allen–Feldman equation.23–25 The argyrodites, though crystalline, are strongly anharmonic and quite complex in structure and, thus, can be suspected to draw contributions from both the quasiparticle and diffusive channels. Under the unifying Wigner formulation of thermal transport,26 the off-diagonal contribution of the phonon velocity tensors provides an additional channel to the diagonal Peierls term, together describing the entire crossover in the behavior of κlat⁠. The full statement is
      κl=ℏ2kBT2∑λ,λ′∫qvλλ′q⊗vλλ′q(ωλq+ωλ′q2)(ωλqnλq(1+nλq)+ωλ′qnλ′q(1+nλ′q)4(ωλq−ωλ′q)2+(τλq−1+τλ′q−1)2)(τλq−1+τλ′q−1),
      (5)
      where λ is the phonon mode index, ω is the frequency, n is the Bose–Einstein occupation, and τ−1 is the scattering rate. PBTE is recovered (⁠ λ=λ′⁠) in the limit of small τ−1 and large ωλ−ωλ′⁠, and the Allen–Feldman equation is recovered (⁠ λ≠λ′⁠) for amorphous, glassy solids in the limit of large τ−1 and small ωλ−ωλ′⁠. The latter, the off-diagonal transport channel, is also commonly known as the coherence term. For glassy crystals with strong anharmonicity, the latter part can be comparable to or even exceed the former part. In solving for Eq. (5), v and ω can be obtained trivially from real phonon spectra, but τ−1 requires separate calculation of phonon–phonon scattering rates. Three-phonon (3ph) scattering rates are computed by perturbation theory using the third-order anharmonic IFCs as the scattering potential. These calculations are all done using the Phono3py.27 As implemented in the software, linearized BTE is solved exactly by Chaput’s approach via direct diagonalization of the 3ph scattering matrix28 assembled from 3rd-order IFCs and phonon eigenvectors.29 At each temperature where κlat is computed, the corresponding temperature-renormalized harmonic IFCs are used. Calculation of κlat is determined to be converged by a 12×12×12 q-mesh—see Fig. 5 in the  Appendix.

      III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

      The Li 6PS 5X lattice is cubic and crystallizes in the F4¯3m space group. The S and P atoms are tetrahedrally coordinated. Notably, the Li atoms are bonded to three S atoms in trigonal planar coordination environments, analogous to the threefold planar coordination environment of copper atoms in many low- κlat materials, which is known to entail strong anharmonicity.30 The vibrational mode that displaces Li atoms out of the trigonal plane is visualized in Fig. 1. The energy landscape of the vibration is a double well whereby the inverted harmonic well is stabilized at large distances by a 4th order anharmonic well. In hindsight, such metastability of Li in their equilibrium sites is not entirely surprising since the ease of Li diffusion through the lattice is what produces high ionic conductivity making these materials good SSE candidates. It is nevertheless physically out of the ordinary that by far the lightest atoms in a system dominate the softest vibrational modes.

      The temperature-dependent phonon spectra and density of states (DOS) of all three compounds are plotted in Fig. 2. The original 0 K dispersion (marked “DFT”) shows that Li 6PS 5X have imaginary phonons that the partial DOS plots confirm to be Li vibration. The imaginary modes completely stabilize by 200 K, which allows the materials to be used at room temperature as SSEs. They stabilize at as low as 100 K for Li 6PS 5I. The low-lying acoustic modes are, in turn, dominated by the halogens, which represent the heaviest atoms in these systems. The acoustic modes, while real, are also very soft, all positioned below 10 meV in frequency and, in the case of Li 6PS 5I, below 5 meV. All of these are fingerprints for a low group velocity and strong anharmonic scattering, leading to very low κlat⁠.

      The thermal transport results of the three materials are shown in Fig. 3. All three exhibit very low, nearly temperature-independent κlat whereby the diagonal Peierls term and the off-diagonal coherence term comparably contribute to the total. The predicted total conductivity agrees well with experimental measurements from Ref. 4. The κl≈0.5∼0.7 W m −1 K −1 is lower than that of Li7La3Zr2O12 (LLZO) or NASICON and only slightly higher than that of LGPS. Such glassy κlat with only 3ph scattering accounted for indicates that the ultimate result will remain very similar if four-phonon scattering is included. Four-phonon scattering would further decrease the Peierls part but commensurately increase the coherence part, overall maintaining the low total κlat and its largely temperature-independent trend if not push it to a slightly more increasing trend. The remaining discrepancy between calculated and measured values could be due to multiple reasons. Filling fraction of samples by the actual electrolyte is often not 100%, which deviates from the structural ideality assumed in calculations.

      The reason that κP somewhat increases in some temperature ranges, most notably between 200 and 300 K for Li 6PS 5I, is a direct consequence of us using the phonon dispersion renormalized at each temperature for that temperature. For a given phonon dispersion, κP must monotonically decrease with temperature. However, in this case, dispersion at each temperature is changing, with the biggest change occurring around 200∼300 K, where the imaginary optical modes have just turned real.

      Though all three compounds exhibit very similar values, close examination reveals that the lightest Li 6PS 5Cl exhibits the highest κlat⁠, Li 6PS 5Br exhibits the lowest κlat⁠, and the heaviest Li 6PS 5I exhibits intermediate κlat⁠. Though the differences are only by the slightest measure, the order is consistent between experiment and our computation. The order is also somewhat out of the ordinary since the middle compound in mass is the one to attain the lowest κlat and, as Fig. 3(b) shows, the largest contribution from the coherence effect. Referring back to the phonon dispersion and DOS in Fig. 2(b), the Br atoms form the sharpest and most localized (flattest) acoustic modes of the three halogen atoms. This leads to Li 6PS 5Br drawing the largest (smallest) relative contribution from the coherence (Peierls) term, for the lowest overall κlat⁠.

      For a more microscopic understanding, we plot group velocities and phonon mean free paths (MFPs) in Fig. 4. Figures 4(a)–4(c) show that the acoustic modes in all three compounds are so soft that they do not offer any meaningfully higher velocity than the optical modes. The average acoustic velocity at long wavelengths is around 1500 m/s, which is very low for crystalline solids. As acoustic modes are the usual heat carriers, their blunt dispersion prevents the argyrodite systems from attaining high κlat⁠. Phonon mean free paths (MFPs) plotted in Figs. 4(d)–4(f) are computed with lifetimes from the relaxation-time approximation. Notably, there is hardly any phonon exceeding 10 nm in MFP, which is the typical length scale of the smallest nanostructures. In particular, for Li 6PS 5Br, no mode exceeds 10 nm in MFP, indicating the strongest scattering of all and consistent with the highest coherence contribution of all. The short MFPs suggest that these argyrodites would exhibit similarly low κlat no matter how small the grain size, which is difficult to control during battery operation.

      It is known that in superionic solid-state electrolytes, Li + transport may contribute to κlat⁠.31–33 The temperature range of concern in our study, however, is too low for the ions to contribute significantly. The ion contribution to κlat in Ref. 33 was found to be substantial at above 1100 K and up to 46% of the total thermal conductivity T=1300K⁠. Li + hopping conductivity increases exponentially as temperature, meaning at 500 K, it will be lower by an order of magnitude. The exact ratio depends on the hopping barrier height, but assuming a usual κlat hopping barrier of 0.25 eV in electrolytes, the conductivity at 500 K is lower by 40 times than at 1300 K, meaning that the ion will be an insignificant contributor to κlat⁠. That ion transport is not significant for thermal transport at lower temperatures is also established by other studies.5 As a testament in this study, the Wigner formulation is able to predict the increase in κlat with the temperature in Li 6PS5 I owing to the coherence effect.

      By any expectation, electrons affect thermal transport in these systems negligibly. There are two ways in which electrons can affect thermal transport: one via electronic thermal conductivity and two via phonon–electron scattering. Electronic (thermal) conductivity is very low in these systems, which is why they are considered solid-state electrolytes. Phonon–electron scattering will be eclipsed by anharmonic phonon–phonon scattering in such systems as κlat is already attaining the glass limit.

      IV. CONCLUSION

      Glassy thermal conductivities of Li 6PS 5X argyrodites are computed. Good agreements with experimental measurements are found at the level of unified Wigner transport formalism and direct diagonalization of the scattering matrix, without molecular dynamics and the Green–Kubo equation that are often employed for SSEs. Three-phonon scattering is sufficient to suppress thermal conductivity to the glassy level. Dynamical instability in these systems is caused by the Li vibration out of its trigonal plane. This is resolved with accurate anharmonic force constants and effective harmonic renormalization using temperature-dependent ensembles generated by quantum covariance sampling. As these argyrodites are expected to be poor thermal dissipators in all forms, their use as SSEs would require careful thermal management. If heat dissipates slowly in a battery, it can accumulate quickly, leading to performance degradation and even thermal runaway,34 resulting in battery failure.
